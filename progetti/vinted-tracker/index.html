Vinted Tracker

A program that hunts for bargains on Vinted for me.

Role On my own·finished

Vinted Tracker is a program for personal use, built to find deals on Vinted, the second-hand marketplace: listings priced far below what is typical for the same product. It started from a simple task and turned into a much harder one. The first version just sent an alert when a new listing came up for the saved searches. The last one brings together the listings for the same product, written any old way and in seven languages, to work out which price is really a deal, and deals are searched for on demand: the listings that ended up in mixed groups went down from about one in four to fewer than two in a hundred. The project is finished, and the code of the last version is on GitHub.

The typical price and product groups

In the definition of a deal, the hard part is "the same product". For example, to know what a 27-inch 4K Samsung monitor usually costs, you first have to find all the listings about that monitor, and only that one: not the 28-inch ones, not the LG monitors, not the HDMI cables whose title says "Samsung monitor". Almost all the work went there, into making clean groups, with consistent prices.

A group's typical price is the median, the price in the middle: half the listings cost less, half cost more. Around the median, the prices of the same used product spread out in a bell shape, and a deal is a price much lower down, outside the bell.

The first version: alerts on new listings

The first version was a desktop app: each search with its own keywords, minimum and maximum price, brands, words to exclude and how many minutes between checks. When a new listing comes up an alert arrives, and if the Wi-Fi drops, it runs a full scan again once the connection is back.

The stages of the pipeline

In the last version every listing goes through a series of stages, always in the same order, which together make up the pipeline: from cleaning the title to the comparison with its group's price. In the simulation below the stages are the real ones from the code, but the seven listings are made up: the ones from the example above (the 27-inch 4K Samsung in three different listings, the 28-inch, the LG and the HDMI cable) plus an unbranded monitor. The first chapter shows them with title and price. In the last two stages, to show how the prices are spread, more made-up listings are added to the group.

How the quality of the groups is measured

A wrong group doesn't show at a glance: you have to go looking for it. For example, in a Nintendo Switch group, consoles and games had ended up together, and every game looked like a discounted console: the same problem as the cable in the simulation.

To find them I built a yardstick, that is a score that says how much of the catalogue ends up in mixed groups, how much is left on its own and how consistent the prices are. With that yardstick each variant of the pipeline can be tested in a few seconds, and the best ones are confirmed on the whole database, hundreds of thousands of listings. Many ideas were tried and dropped. The one that made the difference is the mixture check, that is stage 4 of the simulation (groups with two products), the last step before the price.

Titles, descriptions and photos

Groups are built on the titles. I tried the listings' descriptions and dropped them, because they talk almost only about condition and shipping. Photos come in later, and only where the prices don't add up, to split a group in which two different items live side by side: used everywhere, they would risk splitting apart the very listings that belong together. In any case the images are never saved, and all that is kept on the photo side is a vector, that is a list of numbers: as with the titles in stage 3, it turns the photo into a point in a space where similar things sit close together.

Sold listings and removed listings

In my view the typical price shouldn't come only from what people ask, but also from what things actually sell for. That's why a continuous check goes back over the active listings. If the page is still there but the buy button isn't, the item is presumably sold, at the last price seen; if the page no longer exists, it has been removed and doesn't count as a sale. Price drops are recorded too.

The last version's interface and the tools

The interface is a web app that runs on the computer and opens in the browser. The searches, deals and categories tabs are in the photos above; the fourth is for exploring a category with a live search on Vinted, looking at each price against its group's median. Clicking a group shows its price bands, the distribution and the sales over time.
