MARCS

Recognising ships that switch off their transponder to fish where they shouldn't.

Role Researcher in a student team: choosing the area, the data and preparing it for training·ongoing

MARCS (Maritime Activity Recognition & Correlation System) is a research project run by a team of students at PoliMi Data Scientists, the student association for data science and AI at Politecnico di Milano, and its goal is to recognise ships that switch off their transponder to fish where they shouldn't. The project brings together three ways of looking at the sea: the signals ships use to report where they are, satellite radar images and optical ones. I contribute to developing the route model, which uses the ships' signals, in particular to training and data preparation: the dataset, data loading and the pipeline that prepares the data. The project started from the Strait of Sicily, which remains the best setting for testing; because of complications and a lack of data, the work moved to Danish waters, where the data is open. The project is ongoing: two versions of the route model have been trained, at two different sampling rates (one point of the route every few minutes, with different intervals), but testing on real cases has not been done yet.

The AIS signal and the parts of the project

Instead of switching off the AIS signal, someone fishing where they shouldn't can also falsify it. Other team members work on the satellite radar images, which see ships even at night and through clouds, and on the optical ones; another member works on anomaly detection. The route model starts from GeoTrackNet, an already published recurrent network for trajectories.

Choosing the area

In the comparison between the areas, besides confirmed cases of illegal fishing and cloud cover, what counted was how often satellites pass over, the type of fishing and traffic, how clear-cut the boundaries of the no-fishing zones are, the laws and the weather.

Data cleaning and the pipeline

From the Danish data I kept only the columns the model needs (time, ship, position, speed, course), I also removed rescue vessels, and I picked six windows of days across the year, from the seasonal fishing ban, the period when fishing is suspended, to the summer peak, so that the model wouldn't learn a single season.

Reading in chunks and computing the normalisation statistics up front made training fit in memory, with the weights saved at every pass so it could resume. Compiling the training step and preparing the sequences once, on file, brought a training pass down from days to minutes. GeoTrackNet was ported to the new version of TensorFlow by a teammate.

Project status

Still to be done: training on all the periods, and a decision on stationary ships, which make up about half the sequences: whether to keep them or remove them.

  • The starting model: GeoTrackNet, arXiv 1912.00682.
  • The data: the open AIS data of the Danish Maritime Authority.
