Robotic chessboard

A chessboard that moves the opponent's pieces by itself, with a magnet sliding under the board.

Role On my own·on hold

The robotic chessboard is a personal design experiment of mine, beyond software: a chessboard for playing against the computer, which moves the opponent's pieces by itself, dragging them with a magnet that slides under the board. I didn't build it: it exists as a 3D model, as a simulation and as a long list of problems, some solved and some not. What I spent the most time on is working out where the person playing has moved.

The chessboards on the market

Chessboards that move their own pieces are sold commercially, but they cost several hundred euros. I wanted to try building one myself, with a cheaper approach than the small robots some of them put inside every piece.

The mechanism: a bridge and an electromagnet under the board

Under the playing surface there's a bridge, that is a beam that crosses the box from one side to the other as in 3D printers, and along the bridge runs a carriage with an electromagnet: by moving the bridge and the carriage, the magnet can reach any point under the squares. Every piece has a small magnet in its base, so when the electromagnet's coil switches on, the piece stays coupled through the board and follows the carriage, and when it switches off, the piece stops right there. The carriage never lifts, and from above you only see the piece sliding: so rather than an arm, it's a bridge working from underneath. The rules and the game engine aren't in the machine but in a phone app, connected to the chessboard, which tells it which pieces to move.

The starting requirements

  • Magnet: close to the board.
  • Captured pieces: at the sides, not in a drawer.
  • Footprint: square frame, box as small and low as possible (the versions with the shell reached almost sixty centimetres for a twenty-four-centimetre chessboard: too much).
  • Mechanics: motors and circuits inside, with a structure for the mechanics and a shell on top.
  • Documentation: an assembly manual with figures, procedures and checks, before any purchase.

The technical problems I ran into

As I went on, problems came up that I hadn't foreseen at the start. These are the ones that changed the project.

How the chessboard recognises the move

The machine always knows where it has put its own pieces, because it moved them itself. What it doesn't know is what the person on the other side does: which piece they picked up, where they put it down, whether they knocked into another one in the meantime. Without this information it can't reply, and if it gets it wrong even once the whole game goes out of sync. There were quite a few ideas: in the tool below you can try them one by one, with what was good about each and the reason why, on its own, it wasn't enough.

How far the experiment got

The experiment went as far as it can go without building anything. The next step was a real test bench, with a magnetometer costing a few euros (a sensor that measures the magnetic field, like the ones considered for recognising the move) and an afternoon of measurements, to see whether the simulation's numbers hold up outside the computer too. I didn't buy the mechanics, and of the parts only the magnets arrived. Since the end of August the project has been on hold, and the test bench is the first thing I would start again from.
